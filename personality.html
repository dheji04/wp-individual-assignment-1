Personality

My Strongest Traits & Hobbies

1. Reliability & Discipline

In my academic life, reliability shows up in the way I keep up with lectures, assignments and practical work without waiting until the last minute. For example, during a group web project I made sure my section was completed before the deadline, which allowed the team to revise and polish the final submission together. That habit of preparation is what keeps my work consistent and reduces stress.

2. Analytical Curiosity

My analytical side is strongest when I break a problem into smaller parts and look for patterns. In class, that has meant checking data sets, testing formulas, and asking why a result changed before I accepted the answer. This mindset is useful in both programming and data analysis because it pushes me from “the output is wrong” to “I can trace the cause and improve it.”

3. Collaborative Spirit

I work well in teams because I listen first and then contribute practical ideas. In school activities and projects, I have learned that shared responsibility makes assignments stronger, especially when everyone can explain their part clearly. That collaborative habit helps me contribute to group work while still staying accountable for my own tasks.

Hobbies that reinforce these traits

  • Swimming & water safety – reinforces discipline, calm under pressure, and attention to detail.
  • Exploring data sets for fun – keeps analytical skills sharp outside of class.
  • Community service – strengthens collaboration and empathy, both useful when working on team projects.

My Hero & Guiding Motto

“To get something you never had, you have to do something you never did”

My greatest personal hero is my mother, who has shown me how consistent effort, patience and faith can carry a family through difficult periods. She has worked hard to balance responsibility, care for others, and keep moving forward even when life is demanding. Her example taught me that long-term success is not built on sudden brilliance, but on disciplined habits that I can maintain over time.

This mindset is why I treat every assignment, course, and skill-building task as part of a larger plan. I do not look only for immediate results; I look for the habits that will still be useful years later.

LLMs as a Tool, Not a Crutch

Large Language Models such as ChatGPT, Claude and GitHub Copilot are powerful assistants. Used wisely they accelerate learning; used carelessly they replace it (Mollick, 2023). As a Computing Information Systems student I see them the same way a carpenter sees a power drill: the tool multiplies skill that already exists; it cannot create skill that is absent.

When I use an LLM I treat it as a research partner and a debugger, never as the author of my final work. I typically use it in three concrete ways: first, to explain a concept I have already tried to understand from my notes or course materials; second, to suggest alternative examples when a topic feels abstract; and third, to check edge cases in code before I submit a project. For example, when I was working on a small HTML/CSS assignment, I used an AI tool to compare my structure against a cleaner semantic layout and to test whether my page still met the required headings and document flow.

Because I still have to explain the result myself, the model acts as a second opinion rather than a shortcut. I therefore follow a personal rule: I must be able to explain every line of code or every paragraph of writing without looking at the AI output. If I cannot, I rewrite it until I can. This is consistent with the idea that AI should augment human judgment rather than replace it (Mollick, 2023).

In short, LLMs expand what a prepared student can accomplish; they cannot substitute for preparation. Used as a tool they enhance learning; used as a crutch they quietly erode it. The successful approach is to use AI to explore ideas, test assumptions, and refine communication while keeping the final understanding rooted in my own work.
