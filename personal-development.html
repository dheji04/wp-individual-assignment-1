Personal Development

Reflections on two chapters from George S. Clason’s The Richest Man in Babylon.

Seven Cures for a Lean Purse · The Five Laws of Gold

Seven Cures for a Lean Purse

Arkad teaches that wealth is not accidental; it is the result of habits that anyone can practise. Reading the seven cures made me examine my own small income streams (part-time work, stipends, gifts) and ask whether I was treating them as temporary spending money or as the seed of future security.

1. Start thy purse to fattening

The first and simplest rule is to keep at least one-tenth of every amount received which is the foundation of everything else. As a student, I can begin immediately: every payment goes into a separate “future” account or even a physical envelope before any other expense is considered. The psychological shift from “I have nothing left” to “I already paid myself” is more powerful than the actual dollar amount.

2. Control thy expenditures

Necessary expenses expand to fill whatever income is available unless a conscious budget intervenes. I now track every purchase for a full month so the leaks like impulse food, unused subscriptions, and small convenience purchases, become visible. Once they are visible, they can be cut or reduced, protecting the one-tenth that is fattening the purse.

3. Make thy gold multiply

Money that sits idle is only potential. Once a small emergency fund exists, the next step is to place savings where they can earn a modest return, whether that is a high-interest savings account, a government bond, or a carefully chosen beginner investment. The goal is not overnight riches; it is a stream of income that continues even when I am studying or sleeping.

4. Guard thy treasures from loss

Arkad’s early loss on the jewel venture is a permanent warning: never entrust money to a field or a person whose competence I have not verified. For me, that means refusing “hot tips,” avoiding businesses I do not understand, and seeking advice from people who have already managed money successfully.

5. Make of thy dwelling a profitable investment

Owning (or steadily moving toward owning) the roof over my head reduces fixed costs and builds equity. Even while renting, I can treat my living space as an asset: keeping it orderly, improving it, and planning for eventual ownership so that housing becomes an investment rather than a pure expense.

6. Insure a future income

Compound growth turns small, regular deposits into meaningful future security. Starting a retirement-style habit now, while expenses are still relatively low, is the cheapest insurance I can buy against a lean purse in later years.

7. Increase thy ability to earn

This is the cure I can apply every day. Every new programming skill, every completed data project, and every soft-skill improvement raises the value of the hours I can sell. The larger the earning capacity, the more powerful the first six cures become.

Biggest takeaway: Financial freedom is a skill that can be learned and practised daily, starting with the next dollar I receive.

The Five Laws of Gold

Nomasir’s story shows that gold without wisdom is temporary, while wisdom applied to even a little gold becomes permanent. The five laws are both a map and a warning.

  1. Gold comes gladly to those who save at least one-tenth — the same foundation as the first cure. Consistent saving creates the capital that later laws can multiply.
  2. Gold multiplies when put to profitable work — savings must be employed, not merely stored.
  3. Gold clings to the cautious owner who seeks wise counsel — never invest alone in unfamiliar territory; ask people who have already succeeded.
  4. Gold slips away from the inexperienced who invest in what they do not understand — stick to knowledge I have earned or verified.
  5. Gold flees get-rich-quick schemes and romantic desires — patience and realism beat excitement every time.

My personal alignment: I will treat every assignment, internship and side project as “gold” that must be protected and multiplied. I will seek mentors (lecturers, senior students, experienced professionals) before making financial or career decisions that involve real risk. Most importantly, I will never again treat money or time as something that simply appears and disappears both are governed by laws I can now name and follow.

Biggest takeaway: Wisdom is more valuable than the gold itself. Once the five laws are internalised, any future gold I earn will stay and grow.
